St Breock Downs to Bodmin Moor, 24 July 2018

The afternoon Peter Bunyard walked on the Downs: measured wind, satellite rain, the shower cloud and ERA5 reanalysis, every half hour from 12:00 to 20:30 BST, with the clouds he saw blacken over the moor at about 4 pm.

Over those cells, a grey shower cloud with rain falling from its base: base from the lowest cloud St Mawgan reported each half hour (3,200 ft, 975 m, from 15:20 BST), top 2.1 km from the Camborne 12 UTC sounding (moist to 2.0 km, dry at 2.2 km). The cloud's shape is drawn, not measured.

  11. 11Peter's account (letter of 24 July 2018): from about 4 pm, about 10 miles to the north-east over Bodmin Moor, clouds gathered and grew darker as rain began to fall there, while the walkers on the Downs stayed in sunshine; then the turbines started. Drawn as a cloud 16 km north-east of the Downs that gathers from 15:30, is blackest at 16:15 and clears by 17:00, with its shadow on the moor and the sky darkening toward the north-east only; the Downs stay in sunlight. The satellite recorded no rain over the moor in those half hours; a 3 km shower inside a 10 km cell can be missed.
  12. 12Woodland: tree canopy cover from Hansen et al. (2013), Global Forest Change v1.11 (30 m Landsat, trees taller than 5 m): the 2000 cover with the loss of 2001-2018 removed, averaged over 0.005 deg cells. Green on the ground rises with cover; a rounded crown stands for each 20 % of cover in cells of 40 % or more, drawn 5 times real height; the shape is a symbol, since the data give cover, not the kind of tree. Mean cover over the map is 6.2 %: the moor and the farmland are open, the woods lie in the valleys.

Grey arrows and moving streaks: ERA5 10 m wind on its 0.25 deg grid (reanalysis, not measurement), interpolated in time to each half hour and in space between cells. Orange (two switches, both off at first): the direction Peter gave in his letter, the wind coming in a straight line from the Atlantic to where the clouds had formed, drawn as a dashed line from the coast at Padstow to the cloud (the satellite shower, or the clouds of his account). The LH-ratio relation (Peter's) gives a speed but no direction, so its speed (3.2 m/s while the satellite rain lasts) is drawn as a solid orange arrow along that line. Drawing that speed along the measured wind instead would assume the condensation drove the wind that blew, which is the question being tested. At the Downs the orange wedge shows the angle between Peter's line and the direction the St Mawgan wind went (black ray), given in the readout. Blue ring (switch on): the inflow mass continuity allows toward a 50 mm/h shower a mile in radius, 0.0019 m/s at 8 km, drawn 1,000 times longer than the wind arrows' scale. All arrows use one scale: 1 m/s = 0.6 km. Terrain: Copernicus DEM GLO-90 at 0.01 deg, heights exaggerated 5 times. Station winds step from report to report; St Mawgan reports at 20 and 50 minutes past the hour, shown in the readout.
